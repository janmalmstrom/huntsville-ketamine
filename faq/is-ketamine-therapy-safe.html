Is ketamine therapy safe?

Quick Answer: IV ketamine is safe for most medically screened adults when administered by a physician using evidence-based dosing protocols — but it carries real contraindications and side effects that require clinical oversight.

Key Takeaways

  • IV ketamine has been used clinically for over 50 years, first as a surgical anesthetic, and its safety profile is well-documented in peer-reviewed literature including systematic reviews on PubMed.
  • The standard sub-anesthetic dose for depression and pain (0.5 mg/kg over 40 minutes) is significantly lower than anesthetic doses, which substantially reduces cardiovascular and dissociative risk.
  • Common side effects — dissociation, nausea, transient blood pressure elevation — are predictable, short-lived, and manageable in a supervised clinical setting; they typically resolve within 1–2 hours post-infusion.
  • Ketamine is contraindicated in patients with uncontrolled hypertension, active psychosis, certain structural heart conditions, and a history of ketamine abuse — proper screening eliminates most serious risk.
  • Roughly 30% of treatment-resistant depression patients do not respond to ketamine; safety and efficacy are separate questions, and both require honest discussion before you commit to a course of treatment.

What Does "Safe" Actually Mean in a Clinical Context?

Safety in medicine is never absolute. Every intervention — including SSRIs, lithium, and lamotrigine — carries a risk profile. The real question is whether ketamine's risks are acceptable relative to its benefits for your specific situation. For properly screened patients receiving physician-supervised IV infusions, the answer from over two decades of clinical research is yes.

The evidence base is substantial. Systematic reviews on PubMed covering hundreds of clinical trials consistently report that sub-anesthetic ketamine infusions produce serious adverse events at low rates when protocols are followed. "Low rate" means numbers, not vague reassurance — cardiovascular complications requiring intervention occur in well under 1% of properly screened patients in published series.

Known Side Effects: What to Expect During and After an Infusion

Ketamine produces predictable, dose-dependent effects. Most patients experience some degree of dissociation during the infusion — a sense of detachment from surroundings that typically peaks around 20–30 minutes in and resolves within 30–60 minutes after the infusion ends. This is not a malfunction. It is the drug working.

Other common side effects include:

  • Nausea — occurs in roughly 10–20% of patients; managed with standard antiemetics administered before or during the session
  • Transient blood pressure elevation — typically mild to moderate; monitored continuously throughout the infusion
  • Dizziness or fatigue — common in the first 1–2 hours post-infusion; you should not drive on infusion days
  • Mild perceptual changes — visual distortions or altered time perception during the infusion, resolving fully afterward
  • Headache — reported in a minority of patients; usually resolves without intervention

Serious adverse events — respiratory depression, severe cardiovascular instability, prolonged psychiatric disturbance — are rare at sub-anesthetic doses and are substantially mitigated by physician oversight and continuous monitoring.

💡 Clinical note: The standard infusion protocol for treatment-resistant depression uses 0.5 mg/kg of ketamine delivered intravenously over 40 minutes. This is approximately one-fifth to one-tenth of the dose used for surgical anesthesia. The lower dose is why the dissociative and cardiovascular effects are manageable rather than overwhelming.

Who Should Not Receive Ketamine Therapy

Contraindications are the other side of the safety equation. Ketamine is not appropriate for everyone, and a reputable clinic will screen you out if the risk-benefit ratio doesn't favor treatment.

Absolute and Relative Contraindications

  • Uncontrolled hypertension — ketamine raises blood pressure transiently; unmanaged hypertension elevates stroke and cardiac risk
  • Active or history of psychosis — ketamine can exacerbate psychotic symptoms; schizophrenia is a standard contraindication
  • Structural heart disease or recent cardiac event — requires cardiology clearance at minimum
  • Active substance use disorder involving ketamine or dissociatives — direct contraindication
  • Unstabilized thyroid disease — hyperthyroidism amplifies cardiovascular side effects
  • Pregnancy — safety in pregnancy has not been established for this indication
  • Current high-dose benzodiazepine use — can blunt ketamine's therapeutic effect; requires careful management

Certain medications also interact with ketamine. High-dose benzodiazepine use may reduce efficacy. Naltrexone (used for alcohol use disorder) may partially block ketamine's antidepressant effects via opioid receptor interaction, though the mechanism is debated. Your full medication list must be disclosed before your first infusion.

⚠️ Important: If you are taking lamotrigine for bipolar disorder, disclose this before your consultation. Some evidence suggests lamotrigine may attenuate ketamine's dissociative and antidepressant effects. Your physician needs to factor this into dosing and expectations.

How Physician Oversight Changes the Risk Equation

The difference between a safe ketamine infusion and a dangerous one is largely a function of who is administering it and how. FDA ketamine prescribing information specifies that ketamine should be used only in settings where resuscitative equipment is available and where the administering clinician is experienced in airway management.

At our clinic, every infusion is supervised by a board-certified physician. Blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation are monitored continuously. This is the clinical standard — not a selling point, but a minimum requirement for responsible practice.

Nurse-only administration models without on-site physician oversight do not meet that standard. If you are evaluating a ketamine provider in the Huntsville or Tennessee Valley area, ask directly: is a physician present during every infusion?

Long-Term Safety: Dependency, Cognition, and Bladder Risk

Short-course ketamine therapy — typically six infusions over two to three weeks — does not carry the bladder or cognitive risks associated with chronic recreational ketamine use. Those risks emerge from daily or near-daily high-dose use over extended periods, not from six supervised clinical infusions.

Dependency risk with clinical IV ketamine is low. The evidence does not support classifying a standard induction series as dependency-forming at therapeutic doses. Maintenance infusions — booster sessions every four to twelve weeks — are used in some patients and have not been shown to produce significant cognitive decline or urological damage at clinical doses in published follow-up data.

Esketamine (Spravato), the FDA-approved nasal spray formulation, has the most formal long-term safety monitoring given its REMS program requirements. That data generally supports acceptable long-term tolerability, though IV ketamine and intranasal esketamine are not identical pharmacologically.

Bottom Line

IV ketamine is clinically safe for most screened adults when administered by a physician at evidence-based doses. Real contraindications exist — uncontrolled hypertension, active psychosis, ketamine abuse history — and proper screening is what makes the difference between appropriate and inappropriate use. Side effects are real but predictable and time-limited. Approximately 70% of treatment-resistant depression patients respond; 30% do not. Safety and efficacy are both part of the conversation you should have before your first infusion.
