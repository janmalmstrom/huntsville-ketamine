Does ketamine work for PTSD?

Quick Answer: Yes — randomized controlled trials show IV ketamine produces significant PTSD symptom reduction in 67–70% of patients, often within 24 hours, including those who have not responded to SSRIs or prolonged exposure therapy.

Key Takeaways

  • A 2021 randomized controlled trial published in Nature Medicine found ketamine produced greater PTSD symptom reduction than midazolam (an active placebo), with response sustained at two weeks post-infusion.
  • Symptom relief can begin within 24 hours of the first infusion — compared to 4–8 weeks for SSRIs such as sertraline or paroxetine.
  • Ketamine targets NMDA glutamate receptors, a completely different mechanism than SSRIs or SNRIs, which is why it can work when those medications have failed.
  • Roughly 30% of PTSD patients do not respond to ketamine — honest candidate screening improves outcomes and reduces unnecessary cost.
  • Veterans and first responders with combat-related or occupational PTSD represent a significant portion of ketamine PTSD patients nationally, a pattern reflected in the Tennessee Valley's large defense and federal workforce.

What Does the Research Actually Show?

PTSD affects an estimated 3.6% of U.S. adults in any given year, according to NIMH research and evidence-based treatment guidelines. Standard first-line treatments — sertraline, paroxetine, and trauma-focused psychotherapy — leave a meaningful percentage of patients with inadequate symptom control. For those patients, ketamine has moved from theoretical option to studied intervention.

The strongest evidence comes from a 2021 randomized controlled trial by Feder et al., published in Nature Medicine. Participants received six IV ketamine infusions over three weeks. Ketamine significantly outperformed midazolam — an active placebo — on the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS-5). Response rates favored ketamine by a clinically meaningful margin, with some participants reaching full remission criteria.

Earlier work from Mount Sinai and NYU — available through PubMed's indexed ketamine PTSD trial database — established that a single IV ketamine dose reduced PTSD symptom severity within 24 hours. The multi-infusion protocol extended and deepened that effect.

How Does Ketamine Work Differently Than SSRIs for PTSD?

SSRIs and SNRIs work by increasing serotonin availability. They are useful for many patients, but they do not address what is now understood to be a core biological feature of PTSD: dysregulation of glutamate signaling and loss of synaptic connectivity in the prefrontal cortex.

Ketamine blocks NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) receptors, which sit at the center of glutamate neurotransmission. This triggers a rapid downstream release of BDNF — brain-derived neurotrophic factor — which promotes the formation of new synaptic connections. In imaging studies, this process is visible within hours.

For patients at Redstone Arsenal, UAH, or Cummings Research Park who think in systems terms: SSRIs recalibrate one neurotransmitter signal. Ketamine resets the plasticity of the neural circuits involved in fear memory and threat response. These are not redundant interventions.

💡 Clinical note: Ketamine does not erase traumatic memories. It appears to reduce the hyperreactive fear response those memories trigger — which is precisely what makes it clinically useful for PTSD rather than simply sedating.

Who Is a Candidate for Ketamine PTSD Treatment in Huntsville?

Ketamine is appropriate for PTSD when standard treatments have been adequately tried without sufficient relief. This typically means one or more of the following:

  • Failure of at least one first-line SSRI or SNRI at an adequate dose and duration
  • Partial response to therapy (prolonged exposure, EMDR, or cognitive processing therapy) without full remission
  • Inability to tolerate first-line medications due to side effects
  • Urgent need for rapid symptom relief — particularly when suicidal ideation is present

Veterans, active-duty personnel, and federal employees in Madison County make up a substantial portion of PTSD patients in this region. Combat-related and occupational PTSD — including that which develops following repeated high-stakes incidents — tends to be chronic and difficult to treat with medication alone. Ketamine does not require verbal processing during the session, which some patients find preferable when trauma-focused therapy has been emotionally inaccessible.

What Should You Expect from an IV Ketamine Series for PTSD?

The standard protocol for PTSD mirrors the treatment-resistant depression protocol: six infusions administered over two to three weeks. Each session lasts approximately 40–60 minutes at a sub-anesthetic dose, typically 0.5 mg/kg delivered intravenously over 40 minutes.

You will remain awake but experience dissociative effects — altered perception of time, mild visual distortion, a floating sensation. These effects resolve within 30–45 minutes of infusion completion. You cannot drive yourself home. You should plan to rest the remainder of the day.

Response assessment typically happens after the third and sixth infusions. Roughly 67–70% of patients show clinically meaningful PTSD symptom reduction. For those who respond, maintenance infusions — often monthly or as needed — can extend the benefit. For approximately 30% of patients, ketamine does not produce adequate response, and other options should be discussed with your treatment team.

Key Facts: Ketamine for PTSD

  • Standard protocol: 6 infusions over 2–3 weeks
  • Dose: 0.5 mg/kg IV over 40 minutes (sub-anesthetic)
  • Onset of effect: as early as 24 hours after first infusion
  • Response rate in treatment-resistant PTSD: approximately 67–70%
  • Non-response rate: approximately 30% — honest screening matters
  • Mechanism: NMDA receptor blockade, not serotonin modulation
  • Coordination with existing therapist or psychiatrist is recommended

How Does This Compare to Esketamine (Spravato) for PTSD?

Esketamine (Spravato) is an FDA-approved nasal spray form of ketamine's S-enantiomer. It holds FDA approval for treatment-resistant depression and major depressive disorder with suicidal ideation — not for PTSD. IV ketamine, while used off-label for PTSD, has a more substantial body of randomized trial evidence for this specific indication than intranasal esketamine does.

IV administration also allows more precise dosing control. Blood plasma levels are more predictable with IV delivery than with intranasal absorption, which varies by individual anatomy and technique. Most of the published PTSD trial data — including the Feder et al. 2021 trial — used IV ketamine specifically.

Bottom Line

IV ketamine produces clinically meaningful PTSD symptom reduction in approximately 67–70% of patients, with onset within 24 hours — a profile that is meaningfully different from SSRIs, SNRIs, or therapy alone. The evidence base includes peer-reviewed randomized controlled trials, not only observational data. Roughly 30% of patients do not respond, and ketamine is a treatment — not a cure — that may require maintenance sessions to sustain benefit.
