How many antidepressants do I have to fail before trying ketamine?

By Jennifer Cole

Quick Answer: Most ketamine clinics require failure of at least two antidepressants, but the clinical threshold for "treatment-resistant depression" is typically two adequate trials of different drug classes.

Key Takeaways

  • The standard clinical definition of treatment-resistant depression (TRD) requires failing at least two antidepressants at adequate doses for adequate duration — typically 6–8 weeks each.
  • Roughly 30% of people with major depressive disorder do not respond to standard antidepressants, according to the STAR*D trial, the largest antidepressant effectiveness study ever conducted.
  • IV ketamine produces a measurable antidepressant response in approximately 70% of treatment-resistant patients, often within 24 hours of the first infusion, based on data from Berman et al. (2000) and NIMH-sponsored research.
  • Some patients who have failed only one antidepressant may still qualify — the full clinical picture, including prior treatments and severity, is what matters most.
  • If you are experiencing suicidal thoughts while waiting for treatment, contact the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline immediately by calling or texting 988.

The Clinical Threshold: What "Treatment-Resistant" Actually Means

There is no universally mandated number written in law. But the clinical standard used by most ketamine providers — and the definition validated in published research — is failure of at least two antidepressant trials. Each trial must have been at an adequate dose for an adequate duration, generally 6–8 weeks.

This definition derives from the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH), which sponsored the landmark STAR*D trial. That study followed 4,000 patients through sequential antidepressant treatments and found that after two failed trials, the probability of remission with a third or fourth antidepressant dropped to below 20%.

Failure of two or more antidepressants at adequate dose and duration typically meets this clinical threshold. For a full explanation of how that threshold is defined and documented, see What is treatment-resistant depression?

Which Antidepressants Count — and What "Failure" Means

A trial counts when the drug was taken at a therapeutic dose for at least 6–8 weeks with inadequate relief. Discontinuation due to side effects before reaching a therapeutic dose is generally not counted as a true failure.

Drug classes that count toward the failure threshold include:

  • SSRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, paroxetine, citalopram)
  • SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine, desvenlafaxine)
  • Atypicals (bupropion, mirtazapine)
  • TCAs (tricyclics, such as amitriptyline or nortriptyline)
  • MAOIs (phenelzine, tranylcypromine) — less commonly used today
  • Augmentation agents: lithium, lamotrigine, atypical antipsychotics (aripiprazole, quetiapine)

Failing two drugs from the same class — two different SSRIs, for example — carries less clinical weight than failing two drugs from different classes. A provider will review your complete medication history, not just a head count.

💡 Clinical note: Bring a written medication history to your consultation. Include drug names, approximate doses, duration of each trial, and reason for discontinuation. This is the single most useful document you can provide — and it significantly speeds up the intake process at any Huntsville ketamine provider.

Can You Qualify After Only One Failed Antidepressant?

In some cases, yes. The number of prior failures is one factor, not the only one. Severity matters. Documented suicidal ideation, multiple psychiatric hospitalizations, or a history of severe functional impairment may lead a provider to evaluate ketamine candidacy even with fewer prior medication trials.

Esketamine (Spravato) — the FDA-approved nasal spray — has a more rigid insurance-based threshold requiring two prior failures. IV ketamine, used off-label for depression, gives licensed providers more clinical flexibility in determining who qualifies.

If suicidal thoughts are part of your current picture, do not wait on a list. Contact the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline by calling or texting 988 for immediate support.

What the Research Says About Ketamine's Effectiveness at This Stage

Berman et al. (2000), published in Biological Psychiatry, was the first randomized controlled trial to demonstrate ketamine's rapid antidepressant effect in humans. Subsequent NIMH-sponsored research reported a response rate of approximately 70% in patients with treatment-resistant depression, with response typically appearing within 24 hours of infusion.

That figure requires honest framing: roughly 30% of TRD patients do not respond to ketamine. It is a well-evidenced tool — not a guaranteed outcome. A reputable provider will tell you this before your first infusion.

Primary research is indexed through PubMed, including the Berman et al. trial and the broader ketamine RCT literature.

Key Qualification Criteria at a Glance

  • At least 2 adequate antidepressant trials (different classes preferred)
  • Each trial: therapeutic dose + minimum 6–8 weeks duration
  • Diagnosis of major depressive disorder, bipolar depression, or PTSD with depressive features
  • Severity factors (suicidality, hospitalization history) may lower the bar
  • No active psychosis, uncontrolled hypertension, or contraindicated substance use

Bottom Line

The clinical threshold for ketamine candidacy is typically two failed antidepressant trials at adequate doses — the same standard used in NIMH-sponsored research reporting a ~70% response rate in treatment-resistant depression. Approximately 30% of ketamine patients do not respond. For those who do, response may appear within 24 hours. That timeline is clinically meaningful for patients who have spent years waiting for a serotonergic medication to work — because ketamine's mechanism operates through NMDA glutamate receptor modulation, entirely separate from the serotonin pathways those drugs target.
