What is central sensitization and how does ketamine treat it?

Quick Answer: Central sensitization is a state of amplified pain signaling in the central nervous system; ketamine blocks NMDA glutamate receptors to interrupt that amplification, often reducing pain within 24 hours.

Key Takeaways

  • Central sensitization occurs when the spinal cord and brain become hypersensitized, generating pain signals independent of ongoing tissue damage.
  • Ketamine is the only broadly available NMDA receptor antagonist used clinically to directly interrupt the glutamate-driven cascade that maintains central sensitization.
  • Published trials show IV ketamine produces meaningful pain reduction in 60–70% of patients with central sensitization-driven conditions such as CRPS and fibromyalgia.
  • Effects from a standard induction series (4–6 infusions) can persist weeks to months; maintenance infusions are typically required to sustain relief.
  • Approximately 30% of patients do not achieve adequate response — a fact that should be part of any honest pre-treatment conversation.

What Is Central Sensitization?

Central sensitization is not inflammation. It is not tissue damage. It is a malfunction in the nervous system itself — specifically, a state in which the spinal cord and brain become abnormally amplified in their response to pain signals.

Under normal conditions, pain serves as a warning. A stimulus triggers a signal, the brain registers it, and the signal fades when the threat resolves. In central sensitization, that feedback loop breaks down. The central nervous system remains in a state of heightened excitability long after any original injury has healed — or even without a clear injury at all.

The result: stimuli that should be painless become painful (allodynia), and stimuli that should cause mild pain cause severe pain (hyperalgesia). Conditions commonly driven by central sensitization include fibromyalgia, complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), chronic migraine, irritable bowel syndrome, and some presentations of chronic low back pain.

💡 Clinical note: Central sensitization explains why patients with fibromyalgia or CRPS often show no abnormality on MRI, X-ray, or standard labs — the pathology is neurological, not structural. This is also why opioids and anti-inflammatories frequently fail these patients.

The Neurochemistry Behind the Problem: NMDA and Glutamate

The primary driver of central sensitization is the glutamate system — specifically, the NMDA receptor (N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor).

Glutamate is the brain's main excitatory neurotransmitter. When pain signaling is prolonged or intense, glutamate floods NMDA receptors in the spinal dorsal horn. This triggers a cascade called "wind-up": neurons become progressively more sensitive with each subsequent signal. Over time, that sensitized state becomes self-sustaining.

This is distinct from the serotonin and norepinephrine pathways targeted by SSRIs and SNRIs. Standard antidepressants and most pain medications do not act on the NMDA/glutamate mechanism — which is why many patients on duloxetine, pregabalin, or opioids continue to experience significant pain. Those drugs address different pathways.

How Ketamine Interrupts Central Sensitization

Ketamine is a non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist. It physically blocks the NMDA receptor channel, preventing glutamate from continuing to drive the sensitization cascade.

When administered intravenously at sub-anesthetic doses — typically 0.5 mg/kg over 40 minutes, consistent with the Berman et al. protocol — ketamine may interrupt wind-up signaling at the spinal cord level. This is sometimes described as "resetting" the pain system, though that metaphor understates the complexity.

Ketamine also appears to promote synaptic plasticity through BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) release, which may help the nervous system establish new, non-sensitized signaling patterns. PubMed-indexed trials on ketamine for neuropathic pain support this dual mechanism: acute NMDA blockade combined with downstream neuroplastic effects.

Conditions Linked to Central Sensitization That Ketamine Is Used to Treat

  • Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) — strongest published evidence for ketamine
  • Fibromyalgia — multiple small trials showing statistically significant pain reduction
  • Chronic migraine with central sensitization features
  • Neuropathic pain from nerve injury or post-surgical pain
  • Treatment-resistant depression with co-occurring pain (frequent overlap in sensitization research)

What the Evidence Shows — And What It Doesn't

For CRPS, published trials report response rates of 60–70%. A 2014 randomized controlled trial by Sigtermans et al., published in PAIN, demonstrated significant pain reduction with IV ketamine versus placebo over an 11-week follow-up period, with some patients sustaining relief for months after an induction series.

Fibromyalgia data is more limited but directionally consistent. A small trial published in Anesthesia & Analgesia found statistically significant reductions in pain scores after IV ketamine compared to placebo, with effects lasting up to one week post-infusion in some patients. Clinics operating within American Society of Ketamine Physicians (ASKP) guidelines typically schedule 4–6 infusions for chronic pain, with maintenance sessions as needed.

Approximately 30% of patients with central sensitization-driven conditions do not achieve meaningful relief from ketamine. Duration of effect varies considerably. These are not reasons to avoid the therapy if conventional options have failed — but they are facts you deserve to know before starting.

Why This Matters for Huntsville-Area Patients Specifically

A significant portion of patients in Madison County and the broader Tennessee Valley work in high-cognitive-demand environments: Redstone Arsenal, HudsonAlpha, UAH, and Cummings Research Park. Central sensitization-associated conditions — particularly fibromyalgia and CRPS — frequently go undertreated in this population because patients delay care, self-manage, or cycle through providers without accessing appropriate referral for NMDA-targeted therapy.

Most commercial insurers in the state — including BlueCross BlueShield of Alabama — also exclude ketamine infusions from pain coverage. Patients should plan for out-of-pocket costs and discuss financing options during their consultation.

⚠️ Important: Ketamine is not appropriate for patients with uncontrolled hypertension, active psychosis, or a history of ketamine or dissociative misuse. A thorough medical intake — including a review of current medications such as benzodiazepines, which can attenuate ketamine's effect — is required before any infusion series. What to Expect From a Ketamine Infusion for Central Sensitization

Pain protocols typically differ from psychiatric infusions. Sessions often run longer — up to 2–4 hours — and some patients require higher cumulative doses than the standard psychiatric 0.5 mg/kg. Protocols are tailored to the specific condition and patient history by the licensed Huntsville providers we connect you with.

You will be monitored continuously throughout. Most patients experience mild dissociative effects — altered perception, a sense of floating — that resolve within an hour of the infusion ending. Driving is not permitted on infusion days.

Pain reduction may begin within 24 hours of the first infusion in some patients, though full response assessment typically occurs after the complete induction series. Maintenance infusions — spaced weeks to months apart — are generally needed to sustain relief in central sensitization conditions.

Bottom Line

Central sensitization is a real, neurologically grounded condition driven by NMDA receptor-mediated glutamate dysfunction — not a psychological diagnosis or an exaggerated pain response. Ketamine directly targets that mechanism and may produce clinically meaningful relief in a significant portion of patients; published trial data for CRPS report response rates of 60–70%. Treatment requires an induction series, carries an approximately 30% non-response rate, and is not covered by most Alabama insurers for pain indications. If you have exhausted SSRIs, SNRIs, anticonvulsants, and other standard pain therapies, a consultation with a licensed ketamine provider is a reasonable clinical next step.
