Ketamine versus TMS therapy which is better for treatment-resistant depression?

By Jennifer Cole

Quick Answer: Ketamine works faster and shows higher response rates in treatment-resistant depression than TMS, but TMS has fewer short-term side effects and is more often covered by insurance.

Key Takeaways

  • IV ketamine produces a response in approximately 70% of treatment-resistant depression patients, typically within 24 hours of the first infusion, based on NIMH-funded research.
  • TMS (transcranial magnetic stimulation) shows response rates of roughly 50–60% in treatment-resistant depression, with effects building over 4–6 weeks of daily sessions.
  • Ketamine acts on NMDA glutamate receptors; TMS works by magnetically stimulating prefrontal cortex neurons — two entirely different mechanisms targeting different biology.
  • TMS is more frequently covered by insurance after 2–3 failed antidepressants; IV ketamine remains largely out-of-pocket, which is a meaningful cost difference for Huntsville patients.
  • Both treatments are non-curative — approximately 30% of patients do not respond to ketamine, and TMS non-responders exist as well; honest outcome expectations matter before you choose.

Two Different Treatments, Two Different Mechanisms

Ketamine and TMS are both approved interventions for treatment-resistant depression. They are not variations of the same therapy — they work through entirely different biology.

IV ketamine blocks NMDA glutamate receptors, triggering a rapid cascade that restores synaptic connections in the prefrontal cortex. This happens within hours. According to NIMH research, this glutamate-driven mechanism is why ketamine produces measurable antidepressant effects within 24 hours — faster than any other known intervention for major depression.

TMS uses a magnetic coil placed against the scalp to stimulate neurons in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. It involves no drugs or systemic circulation. It requires daily sessions — typically 20–36 sessions over 4–6 weeks — before meaningful response emerges.

If you've failed SSRIs, SNRIs, or augmentation strategies such as lithium or lamotrigine, both treatments target depression through pathways those medications don't touch. That's why both are options when standard antidepressants fail.

Response Rates: What the Research Actually Shows

Response rates matter. Here is what published evidence shows, without overstating either treatment.

  • IV ketamine: Approximately 70% response rate in treatment-resistant depression, based on the landmark Berman et al. study and subsequent NIMH-funded trials published in JAMA Psychiatry and the New England Journal of Medicine.
  • TMS: The NeuroStar pivotal trial and real-world registry data show response rates of roughly 50–60% in patients who have failed at least one antidepressant. Response rates decline as treatment resistance increases — the more prior medication failures, the lower TMS tends to perform.
  • Non-response: Roughly 30% of patients do not respond to ketamine. TMS non-response is also real, particularly in highly refractory cases. Neither treatment is a guaranteed solution.

Speed of onset is where the difference is most stark. Ketamine can produce a measurable response within 24–72 hours. TMS typically requires 2–4 weeks of daily treatment before response becomes apparent. For patients in acute distress — including those with active suicidal ideation — that difference is clinically significant.

💡 Clinical note: If suicidal ideation is part of the clinical picture, ketamine's 24-hour onset is a meaningful advantage. A 4–6 week TMS course is not appropriate as a first-line response to acute suicidal risk. Always call 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or go to an emergency room if safety is an immediate concern.

Side Effects and Tolerability

TMS side effects are generally mild. The most common are scalp discomfort and headache during or after sessions. Seizure risk exists but is rare — estimated at less than 1 in 30,000 sessions. TMS does not cause dissociation, and patients drive themselves to and from appointments.

Ketamine produces dissociative effects during infusion — altered perception, a sense of detachment, and occasionally nausea or anxiety. These resolve within 30–60 minutes of infusion completion. Driving after a session is not permitted. With prolonged high-dose use, bladder toxicity has been documented; this risk is substantially lower at clinical infusion doses than with illicit ketamine use, though patients with pre-existing urological conditions should discuss this with their provider.

Neither treatment carries the systemic side effects associated with SSRIs or SNRIs — weight gain, sexual dysfunction, or emotional blunting. That's a genuine shared advantage.

Cost and Insurance Coverage in Alabama

This is where the comparison becomes most practical — particularly for professionals in Huntsville working at Redstone Arsenal, HudsonAlpha, Cummings Research Park, or UAH who are evaluating both options under employer-sponsored insurance.

TMS is covered by most major insurance carriers in Alabama, including Blue Cross Blue Shield of Alabama, after documented failure of at least 2–3 antidepressants and confirmation of medical necessity. A full TMS course may cost $200–$500 out-of-pocket with coverage.

IV ketamine remains largely uncovered by insurance for psychiatric indications. A standard series of 6 infusions at a licensed Huntsville provider typically ranges from $2,400–$4,200 out-of-pocket. Esketamine (Spravato) — the FDA-approved intranasal formulation for treatment-resistant depression — is more frequently covered by insurance but requires in-office administration under clinical supervision and carries a distinct evidence profile from IV ketamine.

⚠️ Important: Insurance coverage for TMS in Alabama requires documented prior authorization and proof of antidepressant treatment failure. Verify your specific plan before scheduling. Do not assume coverage without written confirmation from your insurer.

Which One Should You Choose?

The answer depends on your clinical history, urgency, and financial situation — not on which sounds more promising.

Ketamine has a stronger published evidence base in highly treatment-resistant cases and works faster. If you've failed three or more antidepressants — including augmentation with lithium or an atypical antipsychotic — available data favor ketamine over TMS for that level of resistance. See comparative effectiveness studies on PubMed for full trial data.

TMS is a reasonable first choice if you've failed only one or two antidepressants, your depression is not acutely severe, and insurance coverage is a primary concern. Some patients also prefer to avoid any dissociative experience.

These treatments are not mutually exclusive. Some patients complete a TMS course, achieve partial response, and later pursue ketamine infusions for a deeper or more durable effect. Coordination between providers matters — licensed Huntsville ketamine providers connected through this service encourage open communication with your existing psychiatrist or therapist.

Bottom Line

Ketamine outperforms TMS on speed of onset and published response rates in highly treatment-resistant depression, but costs more out-of-pocket and produces short-term dissociative effects during infusion. TMS is more frequently covered by Alabama insurance plans and better tolerated by patients who cannot manage the ketamine infusion experience. Neither is a cure — roughly 30% of patients do not respond to ketamine, and TMS non-response is real, particularly in refractory cases.
