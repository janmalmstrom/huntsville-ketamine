Does ketamine work for CRPS complex regional pain syndrome?

Quick Answer: Yes — IV ketamine is one of the most evidence-supported treatments for CRPS, with response rates around 70% in clinical trials, including significant pain reduction in patients who failed standard therapies.

Key Takeaways

  • IV ketamine produces meaningful pain relief in approximately 70% of CRPS patients who have not responded to standard treatments, based on multiple controlled trials.
  • Ketamine blocks NMDA receptors in the spinal cord and brain, interrupting the central sensitization process that drives CRPS pain — a mechanism no opioid or NSAID can replicate.
  • A typical CRPS ketamine protocol involves 4–10 infusions over consecutive days, at higher doses than those used for depression; effects can last weeks to months.
  • Not all patients respond — roughly 30% see limited or no benefit, and ketamine does not reverse structural nerve damage.
  • CRPS has the strongest evidence base for ketamine among all chronic pain conditions; the American Society of Ketamine Physicians recognizes it as a supported indication.

What the Research Says About Ketamine for CRPS

CRPS — complex regional pain syndrome — is one of the most painful and treatment-resistant conditions in medicine. Standard agents like gabapentin, duloxetine (an SNRI), tricyclic antidepressants, and opioids frequently fail to produce adequate relief. Among all chronic pain conditions, CRPS has the most robust clinical evidence supporting IV ketamine.

A landmark study by Schwartzman et al. (2009), published in Pain Medicine, found that a multi-day low-dose ketamine infusion protocol produced significant, sustained pain reduction in CRPS patients who had failed prior treatments. A randomized controlled trial by Sigtermans et al. (2009) in Pain confirmed that subanesthetic ketamine reduced CRPS pain scores significantly versus placebo, with effects lasting up to 11 weeks post-infusion.

The American Society of Ketamine Physicians recognizes CRPS as one of the clearest supported indications for ketamine infusion therapy, based on this accumulating trial evidence.

Why Ketamine Works When Other Treatments Don't

CRPS pain is not ordinary peripheral pain. It involves central sensitization — a state where the spinal cord and brain become abnormally amplified in their pain signaling. Once this wind-up process is established, opioids, NSAIDs, and most anticonvulsants cannot reverse it.

Ketamine is an NMDA receptor antagonist. NMDA receptors in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord are the primary drivers of central sensitization. By blocking these receptors, ketamine can interrupt the feedback loop that keeps CRPS pain self-perpetuating. No other commonly used analgesic works through this mechanism at therapeutic doses.

This is also why ketamine can produce relief even in patients who have had CRPS for years. The target — central sensitization — is addressable regardless of how long it has been established, though earlier treatment generally produces better outcomes.

💡 Clinical note: CRPS ketamine protocols use higher doses and longer infusion durations than depression protocols — typically 4–10 infusions over consecutive days, sometimes with mild sedation. This is intentional. The dose required to reset central sensitization is higher than the dose needed for antidepressant effect.

What to Expect: Protocol, Timeline, and Response Rates

CRPS protocols differ from depression protocols in two important ways: duration and dose. Where a depression series typically involves six infusions over two to three weeks at 0.5 mg/kg, a CRPS protocol may involve daily infusions for four to ten consecutive days at doses ranging from 0.35 to 1.0 mg/kg per infusion, titrated to patient response and tolerance.

Pain relief, when it occurs, often begins during the infusion series itself. Some patients report a 40–60% reduction in pain scores by the end of the protocol. Duration of benefit varies — some patients remain improved for several months, others require maintenance infusions every six to twelve weeks to sustain relief.

Approximately 70% of CRPS patients in clinical trials show meaningful response. Roughly 30% do not. Factors associated with better response include shorter duration of illness, absence of significant structural nerve damage, and earlier intervention. Multiple peer-reviewed trials indexed on PubMed support these response estimates.

⚠️ Important: Ketamine is a treatment for CRPS pain — not a cure. It does not reverse the underlying nerve or tissue pathology. Most patients require a maintenance strategy, and ketamine works best as part of a comprehensive pain management plan that may include physical therapy, regional nerve blocks, or psychological support.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does ketamine actually work for CRPS, or is the evidence still early?

The evidence for ketamine in CRPS is among the strongest in the ketamine pain literature. Randomized controlled trials by Sigtermans et al. (2009) in the journal Pain and Schwartzman et al. (2009) in Pain Medicine both demonstrated significant pain reduction versus placebo in CRPS patients who had failed prior treatments. The American Society of Ketamine Physicians recognizes CRPS as a supported indication.

How many ketamine infusions are needed for CRPS?

CRPS protocols typically involve 4–10 infusions administered over consecutive days — a longer and higher-dose course than the six-infusion series used for depression. The extended duration is necessary to achieve the degree of NMDA receptor blockade required to interrupt central sensitization. Your physician will determine the specific number based on your pain history and response during treatment.

How long does ketamine pain relief last for CRPS?

Duration varies by patient. In clinical trials, meaningful pain relief lasted from several weeks to approximately 11 weeks post-infusion in responders (Sigtermans et al., 2009). Many patients maintain relief through periodic maintenance infusions every six to twelve weeks. Ketamine does not permanently reverse CRPS — it is a treatment that requires an ongoing management strategy.

What is the ketamine infusion cost for CRPS in Huntsville, Alabama?

CRPS protocols require more infusions than depression series, so total cost is higher. IV ketamine is not covered by most insurance plans for pain indications. At Huntsville Ketamine Clinic, pricing details are reviewed during your free physician consultation — call (256) 555-0100 or visit our contact page to get specific figures for your treatment plan.

What CRPS treatments should I try before ketamine?

Standard first- and second-line CRPS treatments include gabapentin, pregabalin, SNRIs (duloxetine, venlafaxine), tricyclic antidepressants, physical therapy, and sympathetic nerve blocks. Ketamine is typically considered after two or more of these have failed to produce adequate relief. Patients referred by pain specialists at facilities in the Huntsville area, including Huntsville Hospital, are evaluated on a case-by-case basis.

Is ketamine safe for CRPS patients who are also on other pain medications?

Most patients can safely receive ketamine while continuing existing medications, but a full medication review is required before treatment. Benzodiazepines at high doses may blunt ketamine's effect. Opioids do not prevent treatment but may require dose adjustment. Your physician will review all current medications — including gabapentin, muscle relaxants, and any SNRIs — during the intake consultation.

What does a ketamine infusion feel like for CRPS pain patients?

At the subanesthetic doses used for pain, ketamine produces mild dissociation — a sense of mental detachment or dreamlike awareness — that resolves within 30–60 minutes of infusion completion. Most patients remain awake but may feel sedated or perceptually altered during the session. The experience is temporary and medically monitored throughout. Pain relief, when it occurs, is often noticed during or within 24–48 hours of the infusion series.

Can ketamine help CRPS if I've had the condition for several years?

Duration of illness does not automatically disqualify you, though earlier treatment generally produces better outcomes. Clinical trials have included patients with long-standing CRPS, and meaningful response — defined as a significant reduction in pain scores — has been documented in these populations. A physician review of your specific case is the only way to assess your individual candidacy based on disease progression and prior treatment history.
