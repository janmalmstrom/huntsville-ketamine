What is treatment-resistant depression?

Quick Answer: Treatment-resistant depression is major depression that has not adequately responded to at least two antidepressants tried at adequate doses for adequate duration.

Key Takeaways

  • Treatment-resistant depression (TRD) is clinically defined as failure of at least two adequate antidepressant trials — typically different drug classes such as SSRIs and SNRIs.
  • Approximately 30% of people with major depressive disorder do not achieve remission after multiple medication trials, according to the NIMH-funded STAR*D trial.
  • TRD carries significantly elevated suicide risk — this is a medical emergency, not a personal failure or a reason to abandon treatment.
  • IV ketamine produces a measurable antidepressant response in approximately 70% of treatment-resistant patients, often within 24 hours of the first infusion.
  • Huntsville residents who have failed standard antidepressants have access to physician-supervised IV ketamine infusion therapy locally — no travel to Nashville or Birmingham required.

The Clinical Definition of Treatment-Resistant Depression

Treatment-resistant depression (TRD) is not a vague label. It has a specific clinical meaning: major depressive disorder (MDD) that has not responded adequately to at least two antidepressant trials, each at a therapeutic dose, for a minimum of four to six weeks.

Those trials must be from different drug classes to count. Switching from one SSRI — say, sertraline — to another SSRI like escitalopram does not satisfy the threshold. A meaningful trial typically involves an SSRI or SNRI first, then a different mechanism: bupropion, mirtazapine, a tricyclic, or an augmentation agent like lithium or lamotrigine.

According to NIMH research and the STAR*D trial — the largest real-world antidepressant study ever conducted — roughly 30% of patients with MDD do not reach remission after four sequential treatment steps. That is not a small number. In Madison County alone, it represents tens of thousands of people.

Why Antidepressants Stop Working — or Never Work at All

Standard antidepressants — SSRIs, SNRIs, and most others — target the serotonin and norepinephrine systems. That mechanism works for many patients. But depression is not a single-pathway disease.

A significant subset of patients have depression driven more by dysfunction in the glutamate system — specifically the NMDA receptor pathway. Serotonin-focused drugs do almost nothing for this mechanism. No amount of dose adjustment or cross-tapering changes that biological reality.

Other factors that contribute to treatment resistance include thyroid dysfunction, undiagnosed bipolar disorder, chronic sleep disruption, and inflammatory conditions. A thorough workup should rule these out before a TRD label is applied.

💡 Clinical note: If you've been treated for depression but your provider has never checked thyroid function, ordered a sleep study, or evaluated for bipolar spectrum disorder, those gaps matter. TRD should be a diagnosis of exclusion — not simply the result of two failed SSRIs.

The Suicide Risk That Makes TRD a Medical Urgency

Treatment-resistant depression is associated with substantially elevated rates of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts compared to depression that responds to first-line treatment. This is not a detail to minimize.

If you or someone you know is in crisis right now, contact the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline by calling or texting 988. Available 24 hours a day: 988lifeline.org.

The urgency of TRD is one reason researchers have pursued faster-acting treatments. Standard antidepressants take four to eight weeks to show effect — a dangerous wait for a patient with active suicidal ideation. This is a core clinical argument for ketamine, which produces measurable response within 24 hours.

What Comes After Two Failed Antidepressants

The standard next steps after two failed trials typically include augmentation strategies: adding lithium, lamotrigine, an atypical antipsychotic like aripiprazole or quetiapine, or thyroid hormone. These help some patients. They do not help everyone.

Beyond augmentation, options include electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), and — more recently — esketamine (Spravato), the FDA-approved intranasal formulation indicated specifically for TRD. IV ketamine is not FDA-approved for depression but has more than 20 years of published clinical evidence behind it, beginning with the landmark Berman et al. 2000 randomized controlled trial, available on PubMed.

What Distinguishes IV Ketamine from Prior Treatments

  • Works through the NMDA glutamate receptor — not serotonin or norepinephrine
  • Produces antidepressant response within 24 hours in many patients
  • Approximately 70% response rate in treatment-resistant depression studies (NEJM, JAMA Psychiatry)
  • Typically delivered as a series of six infusions over two to three weeks
  • Physician-supervised in a clinical setting — not a wellness spa or self-administered protocol
  • Effects are not permanent; maintenance infusions may be needed every four to eight weeks

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the clinical definition of treatment-resistant depression?

Treatment-resistant depression (TRD) is major depressive disorder that has not responded to at least two antidepressant trials at adequate doses for at least four to six weeks each. The trials must involve different drug classes — switching between two SSRIs does not meet the threshold. This definition is used consistently in NIMH research, including the landmark STAR*D trial.

How common is treatment-resistant depression in Huntsville or Alabama?

The STAR*D trial found that approximately 30% of patients with major depression do not reach remission after four sequential treatment attempts. Applied to Madison County's adult population, that represents a substantial number of people. Many patients in the Huntsville area — including those working at Redstone Arsenal, UAH, and the Cummings Research Park corridor — have spent years cycling through medications without adequate relief.

What antidepressants must I have tried before ketamine is considered?

There is no single universal protocol, but most clinicians require failure of at least two medications from different classes — for example, an SSRI like sertraline plus an SNRI like venlafaxine, or an SSRI plus an augmentation agent like lithium or lamotrigine. Some providers also expect a trial of bupropion or mirtazapine. A physician review of your full medication history will determine whether you meet criteria.

How is IV ketamine different from the antidepressants I've already tried?

Standard antidepressants target serotonin or norepinephrine receptors. IV ketamine acts on NMDA glutamate receptors — a completely different mechanism. The Berman et al. 2000 randomized controlled trial, published in Biological Psychiatry, was the first to demonstrate ketamine's rapid antidepressant effect, with response occurring within hours rather than the four to eight weeks required by SSRIs and SNRIs.

What is the response rate for ketamine in treatment-resistant depression?

Studies published in NEJM and JAMA Psychiatry report approximately 70% of treatment-resistant depression patients show a meaningful response to IV ketamine. That leaves roughly 30% who do not respond — a fact worth understanding before starting treatment. Response is typically defined as a 50% or greater reduction in depression scale scores, not full remission.

How quickly does ketamine work for treatment-resistant depression?

Many patients notice a measurable reduction in depressive symptoms within 24 hours of the first infusion. This rapid onset is clinically significant for patients with active suicidal ideation, who cannot safely wait the four to eight weeks required by standard antidepressants. A standard course is six infusions delivered over two to three weeks.

Is esketamine (Spravato) the same as IV ketamine for treatment-resistant depression?

Esketamine (Spravato) is an FDA-approved intranasal formulation of the S-enantiomer of ketamine, specifically indicated for TRD and major depression with suicidal ideation. IV ketamine uses the racemic mixture and is not FDA-approved for depression, but has over 20 years of published clinical evidence. The two differ in route of administration, dosing, cost, and insurance coverage — a physician can help you determine which is appropriate.
