What does a ketamine infusion feel like?

Quick Answer: During a ketamine infusion, most patients experience mild dissociation, altered perception of time and space, and floating sensations — all resolving within 15–30 minutes after the IV is stopped.

Key Takeaways

  • A standard IV ketamine infusion runs 40–60 minutes at a sub-anesthetic dose (typically 0.5 mg/kg), not the higher doses used for surgical anesthesia.
  • Most patients describe perceptual effects — mild visual distortion, a sense of detachment, or altered time perception — that stop shortly after the infusion ends.
  • You remain conscious and responsive throughout; a physician monitors your vitals for the entire session.
  • Roughly 70% of treatment-resistant depression patients show meaningful response within 24 hours of the first or subsequent infusions, based on NIMH-validated protocols.
  • Pre-medication with a benzodiazepine is sometimes used to reduce anxiety before the session; your physician will assess whether this is appropriate for you.

What Actually Happens During the Infusion

An IV line is placed, typically in your forearm or hand. Over the next 40–60 minutes, ketamine is delivered at a dose of approximately 0.5 mg/kg — the Berman/NIMH-validated sub-anesthetic dose used in treatment-resistant depression protocols. This is well below the dose used for surgical anesthesia. Your blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation are monitored continuously throughout.

You stay in a reclined position in a private infusion suite. You are awake for the entire session. A physician or trained clinical staff member remains present or immediately available at all times. You can communicate if needed, though most patients prefer to keep their eyes closed and rest quietly.

What You Will Likely Feel — and When

Within the first 5–10 minutes, most patients notice a mild floating or weightless sensation. Perception of time and distance may shift — the room may feel farther away, or minutes may feel stretched. Some patients report mild visual effects: patterns, softened edges, or subtle color shifts. These are expected effects of ketamine's action on NMDA glutamate receptors in the brain.

About one in three patients experiences some emotional shift during the infusion — this may include unexpected calm, brief sadness, or detached curiosity. These experiences are not predictable in advance and vary significantly between individuals and even between sessions for the same patient.

💡 Clinical note: The perceptual effects of ketamine are produced by NMDA receptor antagonism — the same mechanism responsible for its antidepressant action. These effects are a sign the medication is pharmacologically active, not a side effect to be suppressed in all cases.

What Most Patients Do Not Experience

  • Loss of consciousness — you remain awake throughout
  • Inability to speak or signal discomfort
  • Vivid hallucinations (rare at sub-anesthetic doses; more common above 1 mg/kg)
  • Pain from the infusion itself
  • Lingering dissociation beyond 30–45 minutes after the IV stops

After the Infusion: The Next 1–2 Hours

Most perceptual effects resolve within 15–30 minutes of stopping the infusion. You will remain at the clinic for a short observation period — typically 30–60 minutes — before discharge. You cannot drive yourself home. You must arrange transportation in advance; this is non-negotiable for safety reasons.

Some patients feel groggy or mildly "foggy" for a few hours afterward. A small number feel nauseated; anti-nausea medication can be administered during the session if needed. By the following morning, most patients feel cognitively normal. Alcohol and major decisions should be avoided for the remainder of that day.

⚠️ Important: Do not take benzodiazepines, opioids, or alcohol within 12–24 hours before your infusion without explicit physician approval. These can blunt ketamine's therapeutic effect or create unsafe interactions. Notify the clinic of all current medications — including SSRIs, SNRIs, lithium, lamotrigine, and naltrexone — before your first session.

How This Differs Across Conditions and Sessions

Patients receiving ketamine for chronic pain conditions — CRPS, fibromyalgia, neuropathic pain — may receive longer infusion protocols, sometimes 3–4 hours over consecutive days, at different dose ranges. The perceptual experience at those doses may be more pronounced. For psychiatric indications, the standard 40–60 minute protocol applies.

Across a standard six-infusion course, patients often report that the perceptual experience changes between sessions. Session one is typically the most unfamiliar. By session three or four, most patients report feeling less caught off-guard by the dissociative effects. The antidepressant response, when it occurs, generally appears within 24 hours of the first or second infusion — not at the end of the full course.

What to Expect: Session Checklist

  • Before: Fast for at least 4–6 hours (solids); confirm transportation home; disclose all medications
  • During: IV placed in forearm; 40–60 minutes reclined; vitals monitored continuously
  • Peak effects: Mild dissociation, altered time perception, floating sensation — onset around minutes 5–10
  • After infusion: Effects resolve within 15–30 minutes; 30–60 minute observation period before discharge
  • Day of: No driving; no alcohol; light activity only

What Ketamine Is Not

IV ketamine at therapeutic doses is not the same as the recreational use of ketamine at much higher, uncontrolled doses. It is not classified as a classic psychedelic — it is a dissociative anesthetic with a well-characterized pharmacological profile and over 20 years of clinical research behind its psychiatric applications. The FDA ketamine prescribing information and safety labeling outlines the approved indications and risk profile clearly.

Approximately 30% of treatment-resistant depression patients do not respond to ketamine. For patients in the Huntsville area who have already worked through SSRIs, SNRIs, lithium, and other standard options, knowing this number honestly matters. Ketamine is a serious medical intervention — not a guaranteed outcome. Conditions we treat and candidacy criteria are assessed during a physician consultation before any infusion is scheduled.

For preparation standards and what questions to ask any ketamine provider, the American Society of Ketamine Physicians patient preparation guidelines are a reliable reference. You can also browse all ketamine FAQ on our site for additional clinical detail.

Bottom Line

A ketamine infusion produces mild, temporary dissociation — floating sensations, altered time perception, and mild perceptual shifts — that resolve within 30 minutes of stopping the IV. You remain conscious throughout. At the 0.5 mg/kg sub-anesthetic dose used in our protocol, approximately 70% of treatment-resistant depression patients show meaningful response within 24 hours. Roughly 30% do not respond. The experience is manageable for most patients and becomes more familiar across a standard six-infusion course. Frequently Asked Questions

What does a ketamine infusion feel like for someone with treatment-resistant depression?

Most patients describe a floating or detached sensation, with mild distortion of time and space, beginning within 5–10 minutes of the IV starting. The experience is temporary — perceptual effects resolve within 15–30 minutes after the infusion ends. Any antidepressant response, when it occurs, typically appears within 24 hours, not during the infusion itself.

Will I hallucinate during a ketamine infusion?

True hallucinations are uncommon at the sub-anesthetic doses used for psychiatric treatment — approximately 0.5 mg/kg over 40–60 minutes. Most patients report mild visual softening or altered perception, not vivid hallucinations. Higher doses, used in some chronic pain protocols, carry a greater likelihood of more pronounced perceptual effects.

Is a ketamine infusion painful?

The infusion itself is not painful — it is delivered through a standard IV catheter, typically placed in the forearm. Some patients experience mild nausea, which can be managed with anti-nausea medication administered during the session. Blood pressure may rise modestly during the infusion, which is monitored and expected at therapeutic doses.

How long does the ketamine infusion experience last?

The IV infusion runs 40–60 minutes for psychiatric indications. Perceptual effects begin within minutes and resolve approximately 15–30 minutes after the IV is stopped. You will remain at the clinic for an observation period of 30–60 minutes before discharge, meaning the total clinic visit is typically 2–2.5 hours.

Can I drive myself home after a ketamine infusion in Huntsville?

No — driving after a ketamine infusion is not permitted, and this rule is enforced at our clinic. Cognitive and perceptual effects may persist for several hours after discharge, even if you feel subjectively normal. You must arrange a responsible adult to drive you home before your appointment is confirmed.

How does ketamine feel compared to taking an SSRI or SNRI?

SSRIs and SNRIs produce no perceptual effects during dosing and require 4–8 weeks to assess effectiveness. Ketamine produces immediate, temporary dissociative effects during infusion and — in treatment-resistant patients who respond — measurable antidepressant effects within 24 hours, as documented in NIMH and JAMA Psychiatry studies. The mechanisms are entirely different: SSRIs act on serotonin reuptake; ketamine acts on NMDA glutamate receptors.

What medications should I stop before a ketamine infusion in Alabama?

You should not take benzodiazepines or alcohol within 12–24 hours before your infusion without explicit physician approval, as these may blunt ketamine's therapeutic effect. Medications including SSRIs, SNRIs, lithium, lamotrigine, and naltrexone should all be disclosed to your physician — most are continued, but dosing timing may be adjusted. Provide a complete medication list at your consultation.

Is the ketamine infusion experience different for chronic pain patients versus depression patients?

Chronic pain protocols — particularly for CRPS — often involve longer infusions of 3–4 hours over several consecutive days, sometimes at higher dose ranges than the 0.5 mg/kg standard used for depression. The perceptual effects at those doses may be more pronounced and longer-lasting. The evidence base for ketamine in CRPS is among the strongest in ketamine medicine, with multiple controlled trials supporting its use.
