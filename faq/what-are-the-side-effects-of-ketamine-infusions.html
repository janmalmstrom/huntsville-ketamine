What are the side effects of ketamine infusions?

Quick Answer: Ketamine infusions commonly cause dissociation, nausea, and temporary blood pressure changes during the session; most side effects resolve within 1–2 hours of completing the infusion.

Key Takeaways

  • The most common side effects — dissociation, nausea, and elevated blood pressure — occur during the infusion and typically resolve within 1–2 hours post-session.
  • Serious adverse events are rare in supervised clinical settings; a 2015 systematic review in JAMA Psychiatry found no severe adverse outcomes in short-course IV ketamine protocols for depression.
  • Ketamine carries a known risk of bladder damage (ketamine-induced uropathy) with long-term or high-frequency use — not typically a concern with standard 6-infusion treatment-resistant depression protocols.
  • Psychological side effects like anxiety or perceptual disturbances during the session are common but manageable and are not signs of permanent harm.
  • Patients taking benzodiazepines regularly should disclose this before treatment — these medications can blunt ketamine's antidepressant effect by up to 40%, according to published research.

What You Will Actually Feel During a Ketamine Infusion

Ketamine is a dissociative anesthetic. At the sub-anesthetic doses used for depression and PTSD treatment — typically 0.5 mg/kg administered over 40 minutes — it produces a predictable set of perceptual changes. You may feel detached from your body or surroundings. Sounds may seem altered. Time may feel distorted. These effects are not incidental; they are a direct result of NMDA glutamate receptor blockade, the same mechanism linked to ketamine's antidepressant action.

These sensations are temporary. They begin within the first few minutes of the infusion and resolve completely within 30–60 minutes of stopping. Licensed Huntsville providers monitor patients throughout the session to manage any distress.

The Most Common Side Effects, by Frequency

NIMH-funded clinical trial protocols consistently identify the same short list of common effects during IV ketamine infusions:

  • Dissociation — reported by the majority of patients; intensity varies and is dose-dependent
  • Nausea — occurs in roughly 20–30% of patients; pre-treatment with ondansetron reduces this significantly
  • Elevated blood pressure and heart rate — a transient cardiovascular effect that typically peaks mid-infusion and normalizes within an hour
  • Dizziness or lightheadedness — common immediately after infusion as dissociation clears
  • Anxiety or perceptual disturbances — a minority of patients find the dissociative state uncomfortable rather than neutral; verbal reassurance and lighting adjustments are effective management strategies
  • Headache — reported by some patients post-infusion; typically mild and brief
  • Fatigue — expect low energy after the session; plan for a rest period and arrange a driver home
💡 Clinical note: You cannot drive yourself home after a ketamine infusion. Arrange transportation before your appointment. Cognitive effects — slowed reaction time, mild confusion — can persist for 2–4 hours post-session even after dissociation resolves.

Less Common but Clinically Important Risks

A 2015 systematic review published in PubMed-indexed JAMA Psychiatry found no severe adverse events in short-course IV ketamine protocols for treatment-resistant depression. Several risks still warrant honest discussion.

Ketamine-Induced Uropathy (Bladder Damage)

Chronic, high-frequency ketamine use — particularly in recreational settings involving gram-level daily doses — is strongly associated with irreversible bladder damage. This risk is not comparable to a standard 6-infusion clinical protocol. At therapeutic IV doses administered over a defined course, bladder toxicity has not been documented as a significant clinical concern in the published literature. Patients pursuing monthly maintenance sessions should discuss cumulative exposure with their provider.

Psychological Vulnerability

Ketamine is contraindicated in patients with a personal or family history of schizophrenia or psychosis. The dissociative properties that make it effective for depression carry real risk in psychosis-prone individuals. Thorough psychiatric screening before treatment is a patient safety requirement, not a formality. Review the full contraindication profile in the FDA ketamine prescribing information.

Dependence Potential

Ketamine is a Schedule III controlled substance with known abuse potential. In the structured clinical setting used for depression and PTSD treatment, this risk is low — but not zero. Patients with a history of substance use disorder should disclose this during screening; providers may modify protocols or add monitoring accordingly.

⚠️ Important: If you are currently taking benzodiazepines (such as clonazepam, lorazepam, or diazepam), inform the provider before your first session. Research published in the Journal of Affective Disorders found that regular benzodiazepine use may reduce ketamine's antidepressant response by approximately 40% in some patients. Tapering benzodiazepines under physician supervision before treatment may improve outcomes.

How Side Effects Are Managed in a Clinical Setting

Physician-supervised infusion protocols include pre-infusion medical review, continuous vital sign monitoring, and a structured recovery period. Nausea is commonly prevented with antiemetics. The infusion rate can be adjusted if side effects become uncomfortable. A physician or qualified provider is present during every session — this is a clinical environment, not a wellness spa.

How This Compares to Side Effects From Antidepressants

SSRIs and SNRIs carry well-documented side effect profiles: sexual dysfunction, weight gain, emotional blunting, and discontinuation syndrome. These effects accumulate over weeks to months of daily use. Ketamine's side effects are acute and session-specific — they do not persist between infusions for most patients.

That trade-off is clinically meaningful for treatment-resistant patients who have already cycled through multiple antidepressants. For more on how ketamine compares to standard treatments, see conditions treated with ketamine or Is ketamine therapy safe?

Side Effect Summary: What to Expect

  • Dissociation, nausea, and elevated blood pressure are the most common effects — all resolve within 1–2 hours
  • Bladder damage risk applies to chronic high-dose use, not standard clinical protocols
  • Ketamine is contraindicated in personal or family history of schizophrenia or psychosis
  • Benzodiazepine use may reduce antidepressant effectiveness by approximately 40% in some patients
  • You will need a driver — plan for approximately 4 hours out of the day including recovery time
Bottom Line

Ketamine infusions produce predictable, temporary side effects — primarily dissociation, nausea, and cardiovascular changes — that resolve within 1–2 hours in a supervised clinical setting. Serious adverse events are rare in properly screened patients on standard protocols. Roughly 30% of treatment-resistant depression patients do not respond to ketamine, and the clinic will discuss realistic expectations with you before treatment begins.
