How Ketamine Works
How does ketamine work differently from antidepressants?

Standard antidepressants — SSRIs and SNRIs — work by increasing serotonin or norepinephrine availability. Ketamine works on NMDA glutamate receptors, a completely different pathway. It also rapidly stimulates synaptogenesis — the growth of new neural connections — which is thought to explain its fast onset. Antidepressants typically take 4–8 weeks to produce an effect; ketamine often produces measurable improvement within 24 hours.

How quickly does ketamine work?

Ketamine's antidepressant effect typically begins within 24 hours of the first infusion — sometimes sooner. This is one of its most clinically significant features for patients in acute crisis, where waiting weeks for a traditional antidepressant is not an acceptable option. The effect from a single infusion generally lasts days to weeks; a full 6-infusion series extends this considerably.

How many ketamine infusions do I need?

The standard protocol for depression and PTSD is a series of 6 infusions over 2–3 weeks, based on the Berman et al. protocol validated at NIMH. Most patients who respond begin to notice improvement after infusions 2–4. After the initial series, some require maintenance infusions every 4–8 weeks; others maintain improvement for months without boosters. There is no reliable way to predict in advance which category you'll fall into.

Treatment-Resistant Depression
What is treatment-resistant depression?

Treatment-resistant depression (TRD) is defined as failing to respond adequately to at least two different antidepressant medications at therapeutic doses for an adequate duration. Roughly 30% of people with major depressive disorder meet this definition. Ketamine is specifically studied in this population and shows approximately a 70% response rate in controlled trials — significantly higher than adding another antidepressant or augmenting with mood stabilizers.

Does ketamine work if antidepressants haven't helped me?

Ketamine's effectiveness is specifically documented in patients who have not responded to standard antidepressants. Because it works through a completely different mechanism (NMDA glutamate pathway rather than serotonin/norepinephrine), prior failure on SSRIs or SNRIs does not predict failure on ketamine. Approximately 70% of patients with treatment-resistant depression show a meaningful response. About 30% do not — we're honest about that.

Can ketamine reduce suicidal ideation?

Yes. Ketamine is one of the few interventions with published evidence showing rapid reduction in suicidal ideation — often within hours to a day of the first infusion. This is clinically significant because most treatments require weeks to affect suicidal thinking. For patients in acute crisis, this rapid effect can be critical. This does not replace psychiatric crisis care: if you are in immediate danger, call 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline) or go to your nearest emergency room.

PTSD
Does ketamine work for PTSD?

There is growing clinical evidence. Studies at NYU Langone and other centers show significant reductions in PTSD symptom severity (measured by CAPS-5 scores) after a series of ketamine infusions versus placebo. The proposed mechanism is disruption of traumatic memory reconsolidation — reducing the emotional charge of traumatic memories without erasing them. Ketamine is most effective for PTSD when combined with trauma-focused therapy during or shortly after the infusion series.

Is ketamine better than SSRIs for PTSD?

SSRIs (sertraline, paroxetine) are the only FDA-approved medications for PTSD, and they leave roughly 40% of patients without adequate relief. Ketamine is not FDA-approved for PTSD — it is used off-label based on evidence from clinical trials. For patients who have failed SSRIs or trauma-focused therapy, ketamine represents a distinct mechanistic option rather than a replacement for first-line care. It should be considered in consultation with a psychiatrist familiar with PTSD treatment.

Chronic Pain & CRPS
What pain conditions does ketamine treat?

Ketamine has the strongest evidence for Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS), one of the most painful conditions in medicine. It is also used for fibromyalgia, neuropathic pain (from diabetes, chemotherapy, or nerve injury), and intractable migraine — conditions involving central sensitization, where the nervous system amplifies pain signals. Ketamine interrupts this amplification via NMDA receptor blockade. Pain protocols typically require higher doses or longer infusion times than psychiatric protocols.

How long does pain relief from ketamine last?

Duration varies by condition and individual. For CRPS, published studies show relief lasting weeks to months after an infusion series; some patients achieve sustained relief with periodic maintenance infusions. Fibromyalgia and neuropathic pain responses tend to be shorter-lived. There are no guarantees — approximately 30% of chronic pain patients do not respond meaningfully. For patients who do respond, ketamine can meaningfully reduce reliance on opioids and other analgesics.

The Infusion Experience
What does a ketamine infusion feel like?

Ketamine produces a dissociative state — a sense of detachment from your body and surroundings. Most patients describe it as dreamlike, with visual and sensory changes. You remain conscious throughout but are not operating normally. The infusion runs 40–60 minutes in a private suite with continuous monitoring. The dissociative effects clear within 1–2 hours after the infusion ends. You cannot drive afterward and will need someone to take you home. Most patients tolerate it well; some find the experience disorienting, particularly on the first session.

How do I prepare for a ketamine infusion?

Fast for 4–6 hours before your infusion (no food or drink except small sips of water for medications). Do not take sedatives or benzodiazepines the morning of unless specifically instructed. Arrange a driver — you cannot drive for the rest of the day. Wear comfortable, loose clothing. Bring a playlist or an eye mask if you prefer a quieter experience (many patients do). Your physician will give you specific pre-infusion instructions based on your medications and medical history.

Can I take my psychiatric medications during ketamine treatment?

Most psychiatric medications are continued during a ketamine series. Some combinations require adjustment or timing considerations — notably lithium (may lower seizure threshold), benzodiazepines (may blunt ketamine's effect), and certain MAOIs. Your physician will review your complete medication list before the first infusion and advise on any changes. Do not stop or change medications without guidance from both your prescribing physician and the ketamine clinic.

Safety & Side Effects
Is ketamine therapy safe?

Ketamine has been used as an anesthetic in medicine for over 50 years. At the sub-anesthetic doses used in psychiatric and pain therapy, the safety profile is well established. Common side effects during infusion — dissociation, nausea, elevated blood pressure, increased heart rate — are monitored and managed by the physician. Serious adverse events are rare in a proper clinical setting with board-certified physician oversight. Ketamine is not appropriate for everyone: contraindications include uncontrolled hypertension, active psychosis, and certain cardiovascular conditions. Candidacy is assessed at the physician consultation.

What are the side effects of ketamine infusions?

During the infusion: dissociation, nausea, elevated blood pressure, increased heart rate, dizziness. These are transient and resolve within 1–2 hours. After the infusion: fatigue, mild headache, temporary cognitive fogginess — typically clear by the following day. Long-term concern: with repeated infusions over months to years, there is a documented risk of ketamine-associated uropathy (bladder damage). This is rare at therapeutic doses with appropriate monitoring but is a real consideration for patients considering long-term maintenance therapy. Your physician will discuss this with you.

Is ketamine addictive?

Ketamine has abuse potential — it is a Schedule III controlled substance. Recreational misuse at high doses over sustained periods is associated with physical dependence and bladder damage. At the sub-anesthetic doses and frequencies used in clinical therapy (6 infusions over 2–3 weeks, followed by periodic maintenance), dependence is not commonly observed. Patients with a personal or family history of substance use disorders are evaluated carefully; this is not an automatic disqualifier but requires a thorough physician assessment.

Does insurance cover ketamine therapy?

For IV ketamine infusions used in psychiatric or pain treatment, insurance coverage is generally not available. Most major insurers classify IV ketamine as experimental or investigational despite the substantial published evidence base. Exception: Esketamine nasal spray (Spravato) has FDA approval for treatment-resistant depression and is covered under REMS protocols through certified clinics — ask if this is relevant to your situation. For IV ketamine, plan to pay out of pocket. HSA and FSA funds are typically eligible. We do not accept insurance assignment but can provide documentation for potential reimbursement claims.

Choosing a Ketamine Clinic
What should I look for in a ketamine clinic?

Key criteria: (1) Physician oversight — a board-certified physician should evaluate every patient and be present or immediately available during infusions. Nurse-only administration carries higher risk. (2) Evidence-based dosing — the standard Berman/NIMH protocol for depression is 0.5 mg/kg IV over 40 minutes. Deviations should be explained. (3) Honest candidate assessment — a reputable clinic will tell you if ketamine is unlikely to help your specific situation. (4) Coordination with your existing providers — integration with your psychiatrist or therapist improves outcomes. (5) Standard monitoring — continuous vital signs, IV access, and emergency protocols.

Am I a candidate for ketamine therapy?

For depression/PTSD: adequate trial of at least 2 different antidepressants without sufficient response, diagnosis of major depressive disorder, PTSD, bipolar depression, OCD, or severe anxiety disorder, and no active contraindications. For chronic pain: documented diagnosis of CRPS, fibromyalgia, neuropathic pain, or intractable migraine with inadequate response to standard treatments. The only way to determine candidacy with certainty is a physician evaluation. Consultations are free and carry no obligation to proceed.

Is ketamine a cure for depression?

No. Ketamine is a treatment, not a cure. It produces remission in approximately 70% of treatment-resistant patients, but that remission requires maintenance for most people. The duration of effect varies — some patients maintain improvement for months after a single series; others require booster infusions every 4–8 weeks. Ketamine is best understood as a tool in an ongoing treatment plan, not a one-time fix. It is most effective when integrated with psychiatric care and, where applicable, psychotherapy.
