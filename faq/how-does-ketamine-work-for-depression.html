How does ketamine work for depression?

Quick Answer: Ketamine blocks NMDA glutamate receptors in the brain, triggering rapid synapse repair that can relieve depression within 24 hours — even when SSRIs and SNRIs have failed.

Key Takeaways

  • Ketamine works on NMDA glutamate receptors, not serotonin — a completely different mechanism from SSRIs, SNRIs, and most other antidepressants.
  • Clinical trials published in NEJM and JAMA Psychiatry show approximately 70% of treatment-resistant depression patients respond to ketamine infusions.
  • Antidepressant effects can appear within 24 hours of the first infusion — compared to 4–8 weeks for SSRIs like sertraline or fluoxetine.
  • A standard course is 6 IV infusions over 2–3 weeks; roughly 30% of patients do not respond, and ketamine is a treatment, not a cure.
  • Effects in treatment-resistant cases are typically measured in weeks; maintenance infusions are often needed to sustain improvement.

Why Standard Antidepressants Stop Working — and What That Has to Do with Ketamine

Most antidepressants — SSRIs like escitalopram, SNRIs like venlafaxine, even older tricyclics — target serotonin or norepinephrine pathways. For roughly 30% of people with major depressive disorder, those pathways aren't the core problem. That's the population NIMH research classifies as treatment-resistant depression (TRD): patients who have failed at least two adequate antidepressant trials.

Ketamine targets a different system entirely: the glutamate system, specifically NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) receptors. Glutamate is the brain's primary excitatory neurotransmitter. In depression, NMDA receptor overactivity is linked to synaptic loss — the actual physical degradation of connections between neurons.

Blocking NMDA receptors with ketamine appears to interrupt that cycle and trigger rapid synaptic repair. This is not a theoretical mechanism — it has been studied at NIMH and replicated in multiple academic centers over more than 20 years of clinical research.

The NMDA Mechanism: What Actually Happens During an Infusion

During an IV ketamine infusion, a sub-anesthetic dose — typically 0.5 mg/kg administered over 40 minutes — enters your bloodstream and crosses the blood-brain barrier quickly. It binds to NMDA receptors and temporarily blocks them.

That blockade triggers a downstream cascade. Levels of BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) rise. mTOR signaling — a pathway involved in cell growth and synaptic protein synthesis — activates. Synaptic connections that had atrophied in the context of chronic depression begin to regenerate.

The result is measurable antidepressant effect, often within 24 hours of a single infusion. A landmark study by Berman et al. (2000), published in Biological Psychiatry, was among the first to document this rapid-onset effect in humans. Subsequent NIMH trials led by Carlos Zarate, M.D., confirmed and expanded those findings.

💡 Clinical note: The 0.5 mg/kg IV dosing protocol — sometimes called the Berman/NIMH standard — is the evidence-based benchmark used at our clinic. It is distinct from the higher doses used in anesthesia and from intranasal esketamine (Spravato), which uses a different delivery route and molecular form.

How Effective Is Ketamine for Treatment-Resistant Depression?

In treatment-resistant depression specifically, IV ketamine produces a response in approximately 70% of patients across controlled clinical trials — a figure cited in research published in JAMA Psychiatry and reviewed extensively on PubMed. That is a meaningful number given that these are patients for whom SSRIs, SNRIs, lithium augmentation, lamotrigine, and sometimes ECT have already failed.

Response is not remission, and remission is not a cure. Roughly 30% of patients do not respond at all. Among those who do respond, effects typically last weeks — not indefinitely. Most patients who achieve sustained benefit require periodic maintenance infusions.

Ketamine vs. Standard Antidepressants: Key Clinical Differences

  • Mechanism: NMDA glutamate antagonist vs. serotonin/norepinephrine reuptake inhibition
  • Onset: 24 hours vs. 4–8 weeks
  • Target population: Treatment-resistant depression vs. first-line use
  • Administration: IV infusion in a supervised clinical setting vs. daily oral pill
  • Duration of effect: Weeks per course vs. ongoing daily dosing
  • Response rate in TRD: ~70% vs. significantly lower for additional antidepressant trials

What a Standard Ketamine Course Looks Like

The standard induction protocol is 6 IV infusions over 2–3 weeks. Each session runs approximately 40–60 minutes of active infusion time, plus monitoring before and after. You remain awake but will likely experience dissociative effects — perceptual changes, altered sense of time — that resolve within an hour of the infusion ending.

You cannot drive yourself home. Bring someone with you or arrange a ride. Avoid benzodiazepines and alcohol before infusions — both blunt the antidepressant effect. Your prescribing physician should be aware of your full medication list.

Patients at our Huntsville clinic are evaluated by a board-certified physician before any infusions begin. That evaluation includes a review of prior medication trials, current medications, and medical history to confirm you are an appropriate candidate.

Who Is — and Isn't — a Candidate

Ketamine is indicated for adults with documented treatment-resistant depression: typically, failure of two or more adequate antidepressant trials at therapeutic doses for sufficient duration. It is also studied for PTSD, chronic pain (particularly CRPS), and bipolar depression — see conditions we treat for clinical details on each.

Ketamine is not appropriate for patients with uncontrolled hypertension, active psychosis, certain cardiac conditions, or a history of ketamine or substance misuse. A physician evaluation will identify contraindications before treatment begins.

Patients throughout the Tennessee Valley — including many from Madison County, the Redstone Arsenal community, and UAH — have sought evaluation at our clinic after exhausting conventional options. The evaluation process exists to give you an honest answer about whether ketamine is likely to help your specific situation.

⚠️ Important: If you are experiencing suicidal ideation, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) immediately. Ketamine therapy is not an emergency intervention. While ketamine has demonstrated rapid antisuicidal effects in research settings, it must be administered in a controlled clinical environment after proper evaluation — not as a crisis substitute.
Bottom Line

Ketamine works for treatment-resistant depression by blocking NMDA glutamate receptors and triggering rapid synaptic repair — a mechanism entirely separate from SSRIs, SNRIs, and most other antidepressants. Clinical trials show approximately a 70% response rate in treatment-resistant cases, with effects appearing within 24 hours. Roughly 30% of patients do not respond, and maintenance infusions are typically needed to sustain improvement. Ketamine is a treatment, not a cure — but for patients who have already exhausted conventional options, it is one of the most evidence-supported interventions available.
